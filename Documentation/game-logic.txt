10. Ship health display
10.1. Both healths - mine and the enemy's - are on screen from the very first moment of
the game, at 100%, before anything is placed or fired. Players should see straight away
that there is a health factor and that each side has only one ship.
10.2. There is one board now, so the two healths sit side by side as horizontal bars above
it - mine and the enemy's, clearly labelled, each about half the width of the board.
10.2.1. The wording on screen must never suggest a fleet or several ships - this is a duel
of one ship against one ship, so the labels read "Your ship" and "Enemy ship".
10.3. The bar carries the little ship icon in the ship's current colour, and the percentage,
so the ship and the number always match the colour of the ship on the board.
10.4. The bar empties from its far end as the health falls, and its colour follows rule 6.4
- green at 100%, orange at 50%, almost red at 10%.
10.5. Next to each board label there is a ship counter, so nobody mistakes this for the
classic battleship game with a fleet: it says 1 for the whole game and turns 0 the moment
that ship is wrecked. It is painted in the ship's current colour like everything else.
10.6. Showing the health reveals nothing about where a ship is - only how hurt it is.

11. Collision
11.1. Nothing stops the two ships from sailing into the same square. A player who moves onto
the square the other ship is on rams it: both ships are wrecked.
11.2. A collision ends the round with no winner - neither player scores a point (rule 8).
11.3. That one square shows both wrecks, and the crash is played as a crash: the two hulls
run into each other along the course the rammer sailed, the impact flashes and throws out a
shockwave, and the square itself shakes. It must not look like the ordinary sinking.
11.4. The collision is settled before the fire is: a burning ship that rams on the very move
that would have taken it to 0% does not lose - the round is a draw. So a player never loses
on the last step of their own fire.
11.5. Placement is simultaneous and hidden, so both players can pick the same starting square.
That is a ram too - both wrecked, no point, before a single shot. It is not prevented: telling
the second player "that square is taken" would tell them exactly where the first one is.

12. Shooting odds
12.1. A shot is aimed at one square, so the chance it finds the enemy is the chance that ship
is standing on the square you pick. Everything the rules let a player work out about where it
can be is public, and nothing else about it is, so the odds are one square out of the squares
it could still be on: 8 squares left for it is a 13% shot, 2 squares is 50%, and with one
square left it cannot miss.

12.2. The formula.
  Write:
    B       = the 20 squares of the board (rule 2.2)
    D       = the bombed squares (rule 5.3), shared by both ships (rule 2.3)
    N(s)    = the up to 8 squares bordering s (rule 3)
    U(s)    = N(s) - D, the squares a ship on s is allowed to move to (rules 3, 5.3)
    h       = the square the shooting ship is standing on
    F       = B - D - {h}, the squares a shot may be aimed at (rules 5.1, 5.3, and 2.3:
              you cannot bomb the square under your own keel)
    L       = the last square the hunted ship was pinned to in public - the square it was
              seen firing from (rule 5.2) or the square a shell was seen finding it on
              (rule 6.2.1), whichever happened last
    moved   = whether rule 5.4 has since forced it off L

  The squares it can still be on, C:
    (a) never seen - it has neither fired nor been hit:   C = B - D - {h}
    (b) seen at L and not forced off it since:            C = {L}
    (c) seen at L and forced to move:                     C = U(L) - {h}

  The odds:
    chance = 1 / |C|   if C and F have a square in common
    chance = 0         if they have none, or if C is empty

12.3. Why each line reads as it does.
  (a) Until a ship fires it has no reason to move and rule 5.4 has never made it, so it is
  still on the square its owner placed it on (rule 4) - anywhere on the board. A bombed square
  is not a hiding place: a bomb that landed on the ship was a hit, and a hit would have pinned
  it into case (b), so every bombed square is one it was publicly shown not to be on. The
  hunter's own square goes for the reason rule 11.5 gives - the two of them on one square is a
  ram, and the round would already be over.
  (b) A ship that fires is on the square it fired from (rule 5.2), and a ship a shell finds is
  on the square it was hit on (rule 6.2.1). Until something moves it, that is not a guess. This
  is where rule 5.4's cornered ship ends up - one that fired with every bordering square
  already a crater does not move, and stays pinned on the square it fired from - and it is also
  where a ship sits in the moment after a hit, before its owner's next turn.
  (c) Once rule 5.4 has made it move it is on one of the squares it was allowed to move to, and
  it is not on L any more: the forced move takes a square away as well as adding eight. It
  cannot have moved onto the hunter, because rule 11 would have ended the round. A square of
  U(L) that has been bombed since it sailed is out for case (a)'s reason - the bomb either
  found empty water there or found the ship, and finding the ship pins it again.
12.4. Every square of C is as likely as every other. Which square a ship sails to is its own
choice and no rule says anything about it, so nothing may be read into it.
12.5. A ship can be certain and untouchable at the same time. A shot may only be aimed at a
square that is still usable and is not the shooter's own (rule 5.1, 5.3, 2.3), so a ship
pinned by case (b) on its own crater - cornered by rule 5.4 on the square a hit left it
standing on - cannot be fired at at all. The odds against it are 0, not 100. That is also why
the chance stays 1 / |C| and does not become 1 / |C and F|: a square that cannot be shot at is
still a square the ship may be hiding on, so it is one more way to miss, not one fewer
candidate.
12.6. The number is on screen while the player is aiming: the prompt that asks for the shot
carries it, so a player knows what the shot is worth before taking it.
12.7. The odds belong to a gun, so they are shown on that gun's own health bar (rule 10) and
only while that gun is the one aiming. One number on screen at a time, on whichever side is
about to shoot: on your turn you read what your own shot is worth, on theirs you watch how well
they can see you. Nothing is shown while ships are being placed, while a ship is moving after
its shot, or once the round is over - nobody is aiming then. The empty row keeps its place on
the bar rather than disappearing, so the board below does not resize twice a turn.
12.7.1. The odds are read off the board as it stands at the moment of aiming, not as it stood
when the last shot was fired. So a ship that fired with nowhere to go (rule 5.4) is still
pinned on the square it fired from when its enemy comes to aim, and that enemy's bar reads
100% - which is the plainest statement there is of why rule 5.4 makes a ship move.
12.8. The odds must give away nothing the board does not. The hunter's own square is the one
square of C that depends on where the hunter is, and a hunter knows that, so a player's own
odds count it out and match the squares the board highlights for them. The enemy's odds are
counted from public state alone (C = B - D and C = U(L) with nothing taken out): the one square
of difference, read off the percentage on their health bar, would say they are standing next to
the square you last fired from.
12.9. Before anybody has been seen, every usable square but the hunter's own qualifies, so the
first shot of a round is aimed at 1 in 19.
